# rePurpose Voice & Writing Guide

## In one line
The "professor you'd have a beer with." Wry, supportive, self-aware. Direct without being cold. Calm without being detached.

## Who we're writing for
EPR compliance professionals at brands that produce packaged goods. Directors, VPs, and heads of packaging, sustainability, regulatory, operations, and legal. They're deep in technical work, short on time, and tired of being told things they already know.

Write to a smart peer who's busy. Not to a beginner. Not to a bystander.

Two psychographic profiles to keep in mind:

1. **The deeply in-it specialist.** Already working on compliance, often a "one-person compliance team," knows the regs, looking for help executing.
2. **The recently-assigned generalist.** Someone (legal, ops, packaging) who just got handed EPR as another thing on their plate, learning the regs in real time.

Both want calm, specific, expert help. Neither wants marketing copy, alarm bells, or generic content.

## What good rePurpose voice sounds like
- Calm and confident about hard things
- Specific (numbers, names, real situations) rather than abstract
- Comfortable with humor when the moment allows it
- Acknowledges complexity without making it scarier than it is
- Speaks with the reader, not at them

## Sign-offs by use case
- **Prospect and lead emails:** "Heavy pack? We've got it."
- **Customer emails:** "Here for you." or "We've Got You Covered."
- **Regulatory Roundup (weekly customer newsletter):** "We've Got You Covered."
- **Casual emails (Office Hours, follow-ups):** "Here for you." or a context-specific close like "See you there."

## The climbing metaphor system
A throughline across rePurpose campaigns. Use it when it adds. Skip it when it would feel forced.

Vocabulary that's part of the system:
- **Base Camp** = where you start
- **Camps 1-5** = checkpoints (Data Check, State Mapping, California Focus, Fee Review, Final Review)
- **Summit** = the May 31 deadline (or any major milestone)
- **Trail / climb / route** = the work between now and compliance
- **Heavy pack** = the data, the regulations, the obligation
- **"We've got it"** = we carry the operational load

Phrases that are part of the system:
- "Join the climb"
- "Don't climb alone"
- "The trail is still walkable"
- "You don't have to summit alone"
- "Made this climb with 150+ brands"

## Voice rules

**No "it's not X, it's Y" constructions.** This is rhetorical antithesis that feels performative. Restructure to say what something is, not what it isn't and is. Simple negation ("isn't ready yet") is fine when it's plain factual statement. The forbidden pattern is the negation-then-redirect rhetorical move.

**No parallel antithesis.** "Short on time, long on questions." "Not a project, a partnership." These read as marketing copy. Cut them.

**No filler phrases.** Specifically:
- "where the work gets interesting"
- "the answer matters"
- "breakdown" used as a noun ("here's the breakdown")
- "simply"
- "discover" as a verb meaning "find out"
- "unlock" outside of literal locks
- "in today's fast-paced world" and any variant

**No fear-mongering.** Even when stakes are real (fines, deadlines, public lists), the tone is "we've got you," not "panic now." If the copy would make a reader's stomach drop, rework it.

**Use "creators," not "influencers"** for any creator-economy or partnership context.

## Structural patterns that work
- **Lead with a hook.** A quote, a stat, a scenario, a real question. Don't open with throat-clearing.
- **Bold the first words of bullets** to give the eye a way in.
- **Short declarative sentences** carry weight. Fragments are fine when they have purpose.
- **End cleanly.** No wrap-up tagline ("So there you have it"). Stop when you've made the point.
- **Use real numbers**, not "lots of" or "many." 150+ brands. $100k. 4-5 hours per week. 95 categories.

## Tone calibration by content type

**Customer-facing emails (Office Hours, Regulatory Roundup, EPR updates):**
Conversational but informed. The reader is a customer doing the work. Direct value first, light personality second. Sign-off: "We've Got You Covered." or "Here for you."

**Prospect emails (cold lead nurture, deadline pushes):**
A touch warmer and more guiding. Acknowledges they may be behind or wrestling with something hard. Climbing metaphor lands well here. Sign-off: "Heavy pack? We've got it."

**Blog and long-form content:**
Svetlana voice when regulatory expertise is the point. Confident, specific, willing to go deep but still readable. Open with a real situation or a piece of news. Quote real CAA language or producer questions when they exist. End with what to do, not just what's true.

**Marketing copy (ads, landing pages, slides):**
Tightest version of the voice. Every word earns its place. Specifics over abstractions. One idea per line where possible.

**Social and LinkedIn:**
Conversational, peer-to-peer. Educational with light personality.

## A working example
Opening of the SB 54 deadline email sent in May 2026:

> The wait is over, and so is the runway. On May 1, California's Office of Administrative Law approved the SB 54 regulations, which means the rules are live and a 30-day clock is ticking. Producers selling packaged goods in California now have until May 31 (or June 1, since the 31st falls on a Sunday) to submit registration plus their 2025 annual supply and source reduction reports.

What's working:
- "The wait is over, and so is the runway." — vivid, two beats, no filler
- Connected sentences with subordinate clauses, not choppy fragments
- Specific dates and parenthetical asides that respect the reader's time
- "30-day clock is ticking" framed as factual rather than alarming

## A version that's wrong, and why
> URGENT: California's SB 54 Deadline is HERE! Don't get left behind.
>
> The countdown is on. Producers across the state are scrambling to figure out what to do, and the clock is ticking faster than you might think.
>
> Discover how rePurpose can help you avoid penalties and unlock seamless compliance.

Why it fails:
- Alarm bell tone ("URGENT," "HERE," "scrambling")
- "Discover" and "unlock" as filler verbs
- Vague pain ("avoid penalties") instead of saying what specifically
- "Seamless compliance" is marketing-speak
- No specific date, no specific action, no acknowledgment of what the reader actually needs

## Humor in the deck disclaimer (confirmed pattern)
Real unPacked decks pair a straight legal disclaimer with a second, dry-joke disclaimer slide right after it — e.g. "Disclaimer 2: Grappling With New Regulations Can Cause Uncommon Side Effects," styled like a mock pharmaceutical warning label. This is the deck's one licensed moment of straight-faced absurdity before the content gets serious. Keep it dry (deadpan regulatory-humor, not slapstick), keep it to the disclaimer slot specifically, and don't repeat the bit elsewhere in the deck.

## Anonymizing customer voices in decks
When a deck quotes real customers from a workshop, webinar poll, or call (not a named, approved case study), attribute by topic only: `— anonymous customer, on <topic>`. Never attach a real name or company unless that customer has already signed off on being named elsewhere (e.g. Tillamook, Ancient Nutrition). This is separate from named case-study quotes, which do carry attribution.

## Saying "we don't know yet" is on-voice
When a regulation has real open questions (final bonus rates, agreement terms, whether a baseline can be revised), a "what's still uncertain" slide that states the open question plainly is more on-brand than confidently guessing or smoothing over the gap. This is the deck-format version of the voice rule against fear-mongering: uncertainty stated calmly, not papered over and not dramatized.

## A working email closer
From the Tillamook case study email:

> If Steve's story sounds like your situation, join the climb with us. The next step is a 15-minute call where we figure out whether your setup is something we know how to fix. No pitch, no commitment, just a conversation.
>
> [Join the climb →]
>
> Heavy pack? We've got it.

What's working:
- Specific reference back to the case study (Steve's story)
- Climbing metaphor woven into both prose and CTA
- "No pitch, no commitment, just a conversation" — concrete reassurance, three short beats
- Clean close with the system sign-off
