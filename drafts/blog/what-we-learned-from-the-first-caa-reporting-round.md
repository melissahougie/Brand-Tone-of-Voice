Brands who just filed their first CAA reporting round keep telling us the same thing: it was hard, and rarely in the ways anyone warned them about.

June 1, 2026 was the first consolidated annual EPR reporting deadline under the Circular Action Alliance (CAA), covering six of the seven states with packaging EPR programs.¹ Depending on where you sell, you could have owed up to eight separate reports: three simplified supply reports (Minnesota, Maryland, Washington), three full annual supply reports (Oregon, Colorado, California), and two California-only reports (the Baseline Report and the Individual Source Reduction Plan). Colorado and Oregon also offered optional eco-modulation reports for anyone chasing a fee reduction.

If your team felt like it was building the plane while flying it, that's because, in a lot of ways, you were. Some of these programs didn't have final regulations until right before the deadline, and guidance documents and workbooks arrived only days before June 1, when teams needed weeks. That's a timing problem CAA created, not a gap in anyone's compliance process.

Here's what that first round taught us, section by section. Next year's deadline is exactly one year closer than it feels, and the weeks right after a deadline are the best time to fix what broke.

## Do your applicability homework in the off-season

The biggest first-round trap: figuring out *whether* you're obligated at the same time you're trying to figure out *how* to report. Producers were left estimating scope under serious deadline pressure, then scrambling to collect data that, in a lot of cases, simply hadn't been tracked before in the format EPR needs.

Use the quiet months between reporting periods for this instead of the week before the next deadline. Go state by state, SKU by SKU: producer hierarchy, definitions, exemptions, all of it. Settle whether you're obligated well before you're also trying to work out how much you owe.

## Every state asks for different data: find out before the deadline, not during it

Colorado required post-consumer recycled content (PCR) data this round. Teams that hadn't been tracking PCR by material category watched that requirement show up with almost no runway.

CAA did publish state-specific guidance and Report Preparation Workbooks. Many landed too close to June 1 to shape early data collection, though — CAA could stand to publish earlier. But the deeper issue is that state requirements differ in specific, concrete ways, and the only way to avoid getting caught by one is to go looking for it before the workbook drops. Check the Producer Portal on a regular cadence, and start thinking now about what your own data systems, and your suppliers', will need to hand you next time.

## Don't leave money on the table: eco-modulation bonuses are easy to miss

Here's a pattern worth calling out on its own: a lot of producers who made the June 1 deadline still missed savings they'd already earned, simply because the optional eco-modulation bonus reports live in a different part of the Producer Portal, filed under Help & Support instead of the main reporting flow. Nobody pointed you there, so there was no reason to know to look.

What was on the table:

- **Colorado** offered four active eco-modulation bonuses, each worth a 5% dues reduction (capped at $50,000 per producer): source reduction (weight cut per SKU), recyclability improvements, PCR content above category thresholds, and reuse/refill systems.
- **Oregon** offered three voluntary LCA-based bonuses: disclosure alone (capped at $20,000), packaging design improvements (up to 30% of base fees, capped at $50,000), and a switch to reusable/refillable packaging (potentially worth up to $1.5 million over three years).
- **California** will roll out its own source reduction and PCR content bonuses, with amounts announced in 2026 and applied to invoices in 2029 based on 2027 data. Worth planning for now, even though the payoff is a few years out.

Chasing every bonus takes serious effort, to be fair: a producer going after all four Colorado bonuses and all three Oregon LCA bonuses is looking at up to seven separate submissions, SKU-level documentation, and (for Oregon) third-party-verified LCAs. Run the math on your own fee base before deciding whether that's worth it. At scale, a 5% reduction usually is.

## A one-line methodology is a rejection waiting to happen

CAA reads the methodology section closely enough that a single sentence, or a template copied from somewhere else, gets treated as a red flag rather than a shortcut. Weak methodology sections get flagged, sometimes rejected outright, and rejection means starting the report over from scratch.

Building the methodology properly, before the deadline instead of the week of, does more than avoid rejection. It forces you to find your own data gaps while there's still time to fix them.

## Submitting isn't the finish line: the portal still needs watching

Once a report is in, it can't be edited. If CAA rejects it, whether automatically or after review, you're resubmitting from the top, usually finding out through the Producer Portal rather than a phone call. Catch your own mistake first, and you can request the submission be rejected so you can refile. Self-identify and correct it within 60 days, and there's no financial consequence for the error itself.

Some rejections this round weren't even about producer error: CAA is still working out mechanics like how to handle California baseline reports for companies that didn't exist in the baseline year. Whoever caused a given rejection, the response is the same — check the portal regularly after you file, and respond fast.

## Late or wrong costs money

A few numbers worth memorizing: late payments accrue monthly compounded interest on the unpaid balance. Inaccurate reporting can trigger a charge of up to $5,000, or the full amount of the Producer Fees tied to the misreported materials, whichever is greater. Both sit on top of your actual producer fees, and both come from your contractual relationship with CAA, separate from state enforcement risk.

## Invoicing timing is its own puzzle, and it's different in every state

This is the part that trips people up even after the report is filed: not every June 1 report leads to an invoice, and none of them run on the same clock. Quick state-by-state read:

- **Oregon & Colorado**: Already invoiced. Fee schedules published October 2025, invoices out January 2026, 50% installment billing with the second installment due July 30, 2026. Next fee schedules land around October 2026, based on 2025 data, driving January 2027 invoices.
- **California**: The most layered of the bunch. October 2025 brought modest startup-funding estimates (invoiced August 2026). May 2026 brought illustrative *full* program costs, including the $500 million Plastic Pollution Mitigation Fund contribution, a big jump from the earlier numbers. Real invoices land in early 2027, bundling Base Fees, PPMF fees, Reuse Investment Fees, and administrative costs. The final fee schedule follows program plan approval, expected around October 2026.
- **Minnesota & Maryland**: No invoice tied to the June 1 report. First full fee schedule isn't expected until late 2028 (subject to change).
- **Washington**: Will invoice off the June 1 report. Expect a fee schedule late 2026 and early startup-funding invoices in January 2027.
- **Maine**: No reporting was due June 1. Fee timing depends on the stewardship organization selection, for which the state just issued its RFP.

## Before your next reporting cycle

1. **Start your per-SKU applicability file now, while it's not deadline season**: producer hierarchy, definitions, exemptions, all of it, one state at a time.
2. **Find out what each state's next data ask looks like**, especially anything like Colorado's PCR requirement, before the workbook shows up with no runway left.
3. **Run the eco-modulation math.** Even a 5% dues reduction adds up at scale, and now you know where to look for it in the portal.
4. **Rewrite your methodology section like CAA is going to read it closely.** They will, and a strong one now saves you a resubmission later.
5. **Put your invoice dates on the calendar, state by state**, so a bill next January or August isn't the first you're hearing of it.

Every team we've talked to has been doing its best with incomplete information on brand-new programs moving fast. Building the applicability file and the methodology section this summer is what makes next June 1 look different from this one.
