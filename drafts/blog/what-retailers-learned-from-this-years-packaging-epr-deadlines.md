June 1, 2026 was the first consolidated Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) reporting deadline under the Circular Action Alliance. Depending on where you sell, it could have meant up to eight separate reports across six states, each with its own definitions, thresholds, and portal. For retailers and the CPG brands on their shelves, it was a real test of whether packaging compliance already had a clear owner in the business.

A few patterns from this cycle are worth flagging now, since they'll keep repeating every reporting cycle until someone fixes the root cause.

## "Are we obligated?" is not one question.

Most retail and CPG teams treat producer obligation as binary: yes, you owe a report, or no, you don't. In practice, it breaks into at least five separate questions, and each one gets harder than the last:

1. Do you put packaging into the market in a given state at all?
2. Does that activity clear the state's revenue threshold, commonly $5 million globally, though some states layer on their own criteria?
3. Once supply-chain roles are mapped upstream and downstream, which product lines carry the obligation?
4. Which SKUs qualify for exemption once someone does the line-by-line work?
5. After exemptions are applied, does the remaining tonnage still clear the state's material thresholds?

The last question matters more than it sounds. The businesses that came through reporting season cleanly weren't the biggest or the best-resourced. They knew exactly which of these five questions they were answering at each stage, instead of collapsing all five into one early gut-check.

## The format of your data is riskier than the data itself.

Spreadsheet-based reporting has a specific failure mode: mistakes surface one at a time, after submission, instead of all at once before it. A file that looks final can still be hiding errors nobody's found yet.

The costliest mistakes this season weren't exotic. They were basic classification errors: a film logged under the wrong resin type, a "glass" component that turned out to be something else, plus quantity errors that trigger a full report regeneration downstream. The savings were just as ordinary. In at least one case, simply reclassifying a material correctly recovered six figures that had been sitting unnoticed in data already submitted the prior cycle.

Deadline-time effort doesn't catch either kind of mistake. A standing QC pass does: material classification, component quantities, and a fee-reduction review, run on every report, including the ones that look fine at a glance.

## Repeat reporting requires a paper trail, not just a report.

Nobody questions your numbers the first year you file. The second year, regulators and internal finance teams both will. If a material category shifts, or a figure moves year over year, and nobody in the building can explain why without digging through months of old emails, that gap existed the whole time. Filing again just makes it visible.

Before the next cycle opens, ask whoever owns your reporting a simple question: if a number changes next year, can you explain why, in writing, without a research project? If the honest answer is "we'd have to check a few different places," that's what to fix now, while there's no deadline pressure attached to it.

## Canada is not "one more state."

Retailers with cross-border footprints often treat Canadian EPR as an extension of the U.S. patchwork. It isn't. It's closer to nine separate provincial programs, each with its own fee structure, exemption thresholds, and material definitions, and harmonization efforts already underway will rewrite parts of it again. Building Canada-specific knowledge now gives you a head start on that change instead of a scramble once the next deadline forces it.

## Confidence is not the same as correctness.

Several teams that managed EPR entirely in-house this cycle described the process as smooth, right up until a closer look told a different story. Reporting years got confused with data years. States believed to be inactive turned out to have live deadlines. Early-stage fee estimates got mistaken for final ones and quietly became the number a budget was built on.

None of this reflects carelessness. It's the predictable result of a brand-new, multi-jurisdiction system where everyone is building institutional knowledge at the same time, often leaning on AI tools for categorization with no way to verify the output. The quiet mistakes were the dangerous ones, precisely because nothing threw an error or looked obviously wrong.

## Before your next reporting cycle

Packaging EPR touches sourcing, sustainability, finance, and compliance, functions that don't always sit at the same table. The businesses handling it best this cycle are the ones that gave it a single, accountable owner well before a deadline forced the issue. Regardless of how this cycle went, a few things are worth putting on next quarter's agenda:

1. Confirm which of the five obligation questions are genuinely answered, not just assumed.
2. Put a QC checklist in writing, and confirm it runs on every filing, including the easy-looking ones.
3. Ask for a documented paper trail, so next year's numbers don't require an investigation.
4. If Canada is on your map, start building that knowledge now, before the next crunch forces it.

EPR reporting is still new enough that brands, retailers, and regulators are all building the muscle at the same time. Treat this cycle's friction as a diagnostic, not a one-time fire drill, and you'll spend a lot less time scrambling next May.
