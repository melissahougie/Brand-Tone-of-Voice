Swapping a material sounds simple on a slide: cut the plastic, hit your source reduction number, move on. It rarely works that way once a supplier, a cost sheet, and a shelf-life requirement get involved. In our unPacked: Real packaging expertise & lessons from the field webinar, we sat down with four packaging suppliers who make these tradeoffs for a living, no slides, no scripted pitches, for a candid conversation about what's actually working, what isn't, and what they wish more brands knew before starting.

Moderated by rePurpose's Lowell Huffman, the panel included:

- **Saloni Doshi**, CEO and Chief Sustainability Officer at eco-enclosed
- **Brandon Frank**, CEO of Pacific Packaging and host of the Packology podcast
- **Lev Gershwald**, CEO of a sustainable packaging supplier focused on paper alternatives to plastic void-fill, mailers, and pallet wrap
- **Leo Traub**, co-founder of Zenpak

We're sharing the audience Q&A here so attendees have it to reference, and so other brands working through the same material decisions can borrow from it. A note on how to read these answers: they reflect what's working in the field right now, for these suppliers' customers, not a guarantee for your specific product, retailer, or timeline. Packaging decisions depend on your materials, your line speed, and your budget, so treat this as a starting point for your own supplier conversations rather than a substitute for testing on your own line.

## Material Switches: What's Actually Working

**If we move from a multi-laminate plastic to a multi-laminate with a paper layer, does this help with ISR? Is this any more recyclable?**
Most multi-layered films that include a paper layer are still considered unrecyclable, though one proprietary-coated version on the market is curbside recyclable. Where this move genuinely helps your ISR number is the reduced plastic weight you get when paper or foil replaces some of the plastic layers. Recyclability and source reduction are two different wins here, and this switch is mostly the second one.

**Pros and cons of PP vs. molded fiber?**
Kat Smith shared her team's cradle-to-cradle LCA comparing the two: paper came out "better" than PP at end-of-life, but "worse" during production and conversion, driven by water use and eutrophication impact. It's a useful reminder that a material's environmental story doesn't end at the recycling bin. Production impacts count too, and the two don't always point the same direction.

**Does mono-material plastic generally provide positive ROI vs. multi-layer, considering EPR fees, line speeds, and shelf life for foods?**
Mono-material's advantage under EPR is recyclability, not an automatic fee cut, since EPR fees track plastic weight and component count rather than material type. ROI shows up fastest when the switch to mono-material also cuts weight or keeps your line speed intact, and slowest for high-barrier foods that still need a laminate structure to hit shelf life.

**Any examples of switching to recycled paper/fiber and lessons learned for the business case?**
Saloni Doshi (eco-enclosed) pointed to the economics that changed the calculus for a lot of brands recently: "With the dramatic increase in resin prices in the last several months, paper has started to become more affordable... more and more brands have been able to replace that plastic with paper, and that replacement is becoming more affordable than ever, in certain industries." Leo Traub (Zenpak) agreed: "Paper is my favorite right now... there's already the infrastructure implemented for recycling them... there's already a material that's good enough for a lot of applications, only if you spend more time designing it."

**Have any of you performed LCAs on packaging changes to see environmental impact when moving from plastics to other materials?**
Saloni Doshi said the team's default approach is to ask brands what matters most to them first: minimizing carbon emissions, or hitting curbside recyclability, since those two goals can pull in different directions. Moving from plastic to glass or to aluminum, for instance, can carry a much higher carbon footprint even when it's a recyclability win. "We do provide LCAs for all the packaging we're quoting, so that people understand it," she said. The panel also flagged that LCA methodology itself varies quite a bit: some assessments "start at the 50 yard line," while others go all the way back to raw material extraction, so two LCAs on paper aren't always measuring the same thing.

## Where Paper and Compostables Still Fall Short

**What are the requirements surrounding compostability?**
Lowell Huffman walked through where this stands under California's AB 1201: to be labeled compostable in the state, a product needs third-party certification and it needs to become organic compost, not just break down. There's a federal wrinkle behind that requirement too: most bioplastic inputs currently in use don't count as organic under existing federal rules, which puts a lot of "compostable" claims in a holding pattern. AB 1201 has also been delayed roughly a year due to rulemaking complexity, so the honest answer right now is that the rules are still being written. Lowell added that there's room for brands to lobby and advocate while that rulemaking is still open, rather than just waiting to see what lands.

**There's a trend of moving to paper that's labeled non-recyclable or compostable, but is the infrastructure to process it ready yet?**
Not everywhere, and that gap is the whole issue. Erica, from rePurpose's compliance team, framed it this way: a lot of the frustration brands feel around high fees in certain states comes from infrastructure that isn't built out yet, not from being penalized for no reason. Her advice was to think of the fee as funding the infrastructure you want to exist, rather than as a fine, and to keep the 2030 and 2032 targets, where materials are expected to be compostable or recyclable, in view as the point of all of it.

**Are there opportunities for "clear" tape in consumer packaging, or is compostable the only option?**
Brandon Frank was blunt about this one: "We have not been able to find a clear tape alternative that is going to work... all the clear tapes out there, including the one we sell, are either fossil-fuel-driven plastic or a cello tape, which still counts as plastic even if it's dissolvable or compostable... it doesn't count towards your plastic reduction." His recommendation for brands that need security tape: water-activated tape instead, even though it isn't clear. A second panelist backed this up and went further: "I would honestly just never touch tape. None of us touch tape. We have people for that. We have a special supplier."

## Refill, Reuse, and Retail Partnerships

**How can the food industry participate in refill/reuse requirements?**
Food brands have two paths under SB 54: producer-managed returnables, or consumer-managed refill models. Reusable packaging that touches food needs a safety check every cycle, which is a genuine operational lift, so the easiest place for most food brands to start isn't primary food-contact packaging. It's secondary or transport packaging, or a closed-loop setting where you control the whole cycle yourself.

**Ideas for brands setting up refill/reuse systems: are certain industries easier than others?**
Leo Traub pointed to beauty as the category furthest along: "We saw a lot of energy in the beauty industry for refills. I think the customer is ready, there's been a few proven cases already... it's much easier, I would say, compared with any other industry, to push for that system... right now I think the easiest ones I've seen at least are in the beauty industry." His read on why: beauty customers are often already willing to pay more for a product that sits on a shelf at home for a while, which makes a permanent, reusable vessel feel like an upgrade rather than an inconvenience, something that's a harder sell in categories where packaging is purely disposable.

**Have large retailers (Amazon, Walmart, Target) partnered with brands or their supply chain to align vendor standards with EPR?**
Brandon Frank said yes, and that it's already reshaping supplier decisions from the top down: major retailers "dictate a lot of the packaging materials" their vendors use. His example: Amazon eliminated plastic air pillows a couple of years ago after partnering directly with a large paper manufacturer, which pushed that change through its entire vendor base at once. For brands selling through these retailers, that means some of your packaging roadmap may get set for you, whether or not it's on your own timeline yet.

## Source Reduction Plan Mechanics

**What are the legal implications of not meeting the source reductions outlined in the legislation?**
The 25% source reduction target applies collectively across all producers rather than to any single business, so missing it doesn't put your company on the hook by itself. The incentive system is designed to be net-neutral: CAA pays bonuses to producers who reduce plastic, funded by a malus charged to those using more virgin plastic. The exact fee and incentive rates aren't finalized yet, so it's worth watching for updates rather than treating today's numbers as the last word.

**Any updates on the lawsuit brought by 17 states against California, and what it might mean for compliance?**
Guidance hasn't changed because of it: keep preparing for your deadlines as the program proceeds. A lawsuit filing is not an injunction, so there's no legal basis to slow down your compliance work while it plays out.

**How binding are submitted source reduction plans? Are we legally obligated despite unresolved issues, and are the plans made public?**
Submitted plans are non-binding strategic forecasts, but treat them as credible commitments, not a draft you can walk back casually. CAA is still finalizing formal enforcement terms in an upcoming State Addendum. And yes, plans are subject to public disclosure, so if a plan includes anything sensitive, use the Producer Portal's Confidentiality Claims fields for trade-secret data rather than leaving it in the open document.

**Can you discuss the move from the August 1 ISR Plan to the year-end deadline for "agreements with CAA"?**
The August 1 ISR Plan is your forecast of reduction pathways. It's not final, but it isn't a throwaway document either, since CAA uses it to help set fees and bonuses. What's binding is the ISR Agreement you sign by year-end, which covers your annual report submissions and your participation in the fee and incentive system.

**Can ISR plan documents be revised or resubmitted after the August 1 due date, since an earlier meeting said no resubmission was allowed under SB 54?**
The August 1 ISR Plan is a one-time forecast; you don't resubmit it each year. Your actual progress gets tracked through Annual Source Reduction Reports measured against your 2023 baseline. CAA hasn't published a formal resubmission process yet, so for now, treat what you submit on August 1 as your commitment.

**If a product is "dissolvable," is it a covered product needing reduction, or is it excluded?**
This comes down to the material, not the "dissolvable" label on the package. Most dissolvable films are synthetic polymers, and SB 54 treats those as plastic, so they may well be in scope. There's a narrow carve-out for components that are integral to the product itself, but that's aimed at the product, not its packaging, so it's worth confirming scope directly with CAA if you're leaning on that distinction.

No single answer above is a complete packaging strategy on its own. Between resin prices, retailer mandates, and rules still being written (AB 1201 among them), the honest takeaway from this panel is that a material decision now has to account for cost, compliance, and your own supply chain all at once, not just whichever of the three is loudest this quarter.
