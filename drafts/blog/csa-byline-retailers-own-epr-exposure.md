Retailers Have Their Own EPR Exposure, and Most Are Still Treating It as a Vendor Problem
By Alyssa Dizon, Director of Product, rePurpose Global

Most retailers still talk about Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) as something that happens to their vendors. A CPG brand misses a filing, a supplier scrambles to reclassify a material, a buyer gets looped in only when a compliance question holds up a purchase order. That framing was mostly accurate two reporting cycles ago. This year made clear it isn't anymore.

Private label makes you the producer, not just the seller.

Under most state EPR programs, the "producer" of a private-label or store-brand product is the retailer, not the contract manufacturer that filled the package. That means your own private-label lines carry the same reporting, fee, and source-reduction obligations a national brand carries for its packaging, and nobody upstream is filing on your behalf. (Producer definitions vary by state and product category, so confirm your specific obligations with counsel before assuming either way.) More than one retailer with a sizable private-label program found this out mid-cycle this year, after assuming a deadline that applied to their vendors' packaging also covered their own store-brand SKUs. It didn't, because those SKUs were never the vendors' obligation to begin with.

Collecting vendor packaging data at retail scale is a different problem than collecting it inside one supply chain.

A CPG brand's compliance team reconciles packaging data across its own SKUs. A retailer reconciles it across every vendor stocking its shelves, each with a different level of packaging sophistication and a different willingness to hand over component-level detail on short notice. Ask a few hundred vendors for packaging data without a standard format, and you get it back in as many formats as you have vendors: spec sheets, product photos, emails describing a cap "roughly." Retailers who weren't scrambling by June had already told vendors, months earlier, exactly what data to send, in what format, on what schedule, and built that requirement into the vendor relationship itself rather than requesting it as a one-off ahead of a deadline.

E-commerce and fulfillment packaging is often the blind spot nobody assigned.

A retailer's own compliance plan usually covers what's on the shelf. It's easy to miss what leaves the building: poly mailers, void fill, tape, the corrugate box a direct-to-consumer order ships in. That packaging belongs to the retailer, and it's growing as a share of total packaging volume every year online order share increases. If nobody has explicitly confirmed that fulfillment packaging is inside your compliance scope, it's worth checking rather than assuming someone already did.

Store footprint decides your exposure, not your headquarters address.

A retailer operating in twenty states is exposed to EPR obligations in every one of those states the moment product is on a shelf there, regardless of where the corporate office or primary distribution center sits. That means a packaging or merchandising decision made centrally, a new private-label line, a switch in mailer material, a change in a foam insert, has to be checked against every state where the company has stores, not just the states compliance happens to be watching that quarter.

Before your next planning cycle

1. Confirm whether your private-label and store-brand lines make you the producer of record. Don't assume vendor-supplied packaging already covers it.
2. Standardize what packaging data you require from vendors, in what format, and build it into vendor onboarding rather than requesting it ahead of each deadline.
3. Check whether e-commerce and fulfillment packaging is explicitly inside your compliance plan or sitting in a gap between merchandising and logistics.
4. Map packaging and merchandising decisions against every state with an active EPR program where you operate stores, regardless of where the company is headquartered.

Retail is a different kind of EPR exposure than the brand-side compliance conversation most coverage of this topic assumes. Store-brand ownership, vendor-scale data collection, and fulfillment packaging are retail-specific risks, and treating this cycle's friction as a prompt to close those gaps now will matter more than any single filing did.
