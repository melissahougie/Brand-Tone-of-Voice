This is what we're seeing after a season that combined more simultaneous state deadlines, more repeat reporters, and more first-time Canada filings than any before it: the brands that struggled weren't the ones with messy packaging. They were the ones who didn't know exactly what they were being asked to prove, or couldn't show their work when someone asked.

That's a useful thing to know before your next reporting cycle, whether you file with us or with anyone else. Here's where we saw the real friction.

## "Am I obligated?" is actually three separate questions

Most brands treat obligation assessment as one yes-or-no question. It's really three, and they get harder as you go:

1. Do you put anything into the market in this state at all? → This one's fast, and it's the one that should get answered before you sign with anyone, not after.
2. Which specific product lines are you obligated for? → This is where supply chain hierarchy gets genuinely confusing. Licensees and importers need to check both upstream and downstream in their own chain, and a lot of brands don't realize that until a report comes back wrong.
3. Are there specific SKUs you can actually exempt? → This is the deep, SKU-level work, and it's usually where the real savings live.

If your provider (or your own team) is answering all three with one generic form, that's worth a second look. The brands who came through this cleanly were the ones who knew which of the three questions they were actually answering at each stage.

## Your data format is a bigger risk than your data itself

Spreadsheet-based reporting has a specific failure mode: errors surface one at a time, after upload, instead of all at once. That means a "final" file can still hide problems nobody's seen yet. The fixes that actually caught money weren't dramatic. They were basic checks, done consistently, on every report:

- Material misclassifications (an HDPE film logged as LDPE, a labeled "glass closure" that's actually a different material) go unnoticed unless someone specifically checks for them.
- Component quantity errors quietly turn into the single biggest source of "why do I need to regenerate this" requests.
- Missed fee-reduction opportunities sit in the data the whole time. One brand's poly-coated paperboard reclassification was worth roughly $122,000 in savings once someone actually looked for it. Tillamook's Senior Director of R&D put a number on what a properly reclassified filing was worth to them: "$100,000 - that's how much rePurpose saved us in EPR fees for our Oregon credit. They reviewed our filing and reclassified our materials correctly."

Neither of those started as a dramatic discovery. They started as someone running a checklist instead of assuming the data was fine because it "looked" fine.

## If you're a repeat reporter, you need a paper trail, not just a report

The first time you file, nobody asks "why is this different from last year." The second time, everybody does. If a material name changes, or a number moves year over year, and nobody can explain why without reconstructing old emails and chat threads, that gap was always there. Filing twice is just what makes it visible.

Before your next cycle, ask whoever handles your reporting: if a number changes next year, can you tell me why, in writing, without digging? If the honest answer is "we'd have to go look," that's the gap to close now, while it's not deadline season.

## Canada is not "one more state"

If you sell into Canada, it's worth knowing this isn't a single program you bolt onto your U.S. process. It's closer to nine separate programs, each with its own fee structure, exemption thresholds, and material definitions. Canada's Extended Producer Responsibility landscape is also moving toward harmonization in the near term, which will change the rules again for whoever's paying attention. The brands building Canada-specific knowledge now, instead of waiting for harmonization to force the issue, will have a real head start.

## What to actually do before your next reporting cycle

1. **Ask which of the three obligation questions you've actually gotten answered**: market presence, product-line obligation, or SKU-level exemption. Most brands have only answered the first.
2. **Get a QC checklist in writing**, covering material classification, component quantities, and a fee-reduction pass, and confirm it runs on every report, not just the complicated ones.
3. **Ask for your own paper trail.** If your numbers move next year, you should be able to get a plain-English answer for why, not a research project.
4. **If Canada's on your map, start now.** Nine programs take longer to learn than one, and harmonization won't wait for anyone's Q1.

None of this is about who had a rough first season. Multi-state, repeat-reporter EPR is new for the entire industry, not just for any one brand or vendor, and everyone is building the muscle at the same time. Take the four items above and put them on next quarter's task list before next May turns into this May.
