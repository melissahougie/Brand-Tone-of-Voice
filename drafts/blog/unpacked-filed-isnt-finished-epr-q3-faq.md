Filing your ISR Plan didn't mean the questions stopped, it just changed what people are asking. At our recent unPacked: Filed Isn't Finished session, our compliance team took live questions on what's actually next: Maine's rollout, California's fees and baseline mechanics, and the recurring question of who's actually on the hook when more than one name touches a piece of packaging.

We're sharing the full Q&A here for anyone who attended and wants to reference it, and for anyone who didn't and has the same questions. As always, the answers below are for general reference only and don't constitute legal advice. Consult your own legal counsel to determine how these rules apply to your specific business.

## Maine's New Program

**Can you describe what non-compliant packaging actually looks like under Maine's law?**
This isn't about specific packaging types being outlawed, it's about non-compliant producers. Maine's law allows the state to bar a liable producer, one selling covered packaging above the revenue and packaging thresholds, from selling into the state if that producer hasn't registered, reported, or paid the required fees. Most EPR programs reserve this kind of enforcement mechanism. There's enough clarity in Maine's rules today to determine whether you're a liable producer and get your data and internal processes in order before that becomes a risk.

**What data year does Maine's reporting cover, 2025 or 2026 year-to-date?**
The reporting is for 2025 data. What's being collected for Maine, and what the early fees are based on, is your 2025 packaging and sales data, not 2026 year-to-date.

**Is the first invoice from Maine expected in September 2026 for early program fees?**
Not yet confirmed. Maine's DEP had only vaguely indicated Q3 for reporting, with no mention of fee timing, and that guidance still hasn't been published. The current expectation is that reporting for the 2025 data year will land sometime in Q4 2026, and it's still unclear when fee invoicing will actually start.

## California Fees and Baseline

**If source reduction planning revealed that our 2025 report needs changes, what's the right move?**
Log a support case with CAA as soon as possible, that's the right move for now. CAA doesn't yet have a formal revisions process for California, but the more producers submit tickets describing what they found and want corrected, the more pressure there is on CAA and CalRecycle to build one. This isn't unusual: we've seen it happen for several customers whose source reduction planning surfaced errors in earlier reports. Support tickets go directly through your CAA portal.

**Has CAA said when producers will be invoiced for the Plastic Pollution Mitigation Fee (PPMF), and should we be modeling it separately from the base fee and Reuse Investment Fee?**
We haven't gotten full clarity from CAA on exactly how this will work yet. CAA's illustrative fees cover letter states that the PPMF will be invoiced separately from the program fees, but that hasn't been stated consistently everywhere, and no timing has been confirmed. It's an open question we're continuing to raise with them. We'd expect more clarity once CAA publishes the finalized fee schedule by October 1.

**Is there a way to correct or update the 2023 California baseline if we've found errors in it?**
Not currently. CAA has said the law as set up with CalRecycle doesn't yet provide a mechanism to amend a submitted 2023 baseline report. Part of the complexity is that the aggregated 2023 baseline is used to measure the state's collective 25% source-reduction goal, which makes any change harder to implement cleanly. CAA is working with CalRecycle on this, and for comparison, revisions are already allowed in Colorado and Oregon, so there's a reasonable chance California follows suit eventually. There's no clarity yet on timing or process.

In the meantime, if you've identified a correction and your source reduction plan is based on the corrected numbers, you can explain that directly in your plan's narrative, something we've done for customers whose recalculated baseline differed from what they'd originally submitted. We'd also recommend logging a support ticket with CAA describing the issue.

**When is payment due for the illustrative fees California released in August?**
45 days after the invoice is issued.

## Who's Actually Responsible

**For small business exemptions, is eligibility evaluated at the parent company level or the subsidiary level?**
This is a legal question we can't advise on directly, and it varies significantly by state. California, for example, offers different options for claiming a small business exemption at either the subsidiary or the group/parent level, but the right approach depends on how your company is incorporated and how your packaging supply chain and sales actually work. Because both the rules and the underlying business facts can be unclear, this is a good one to bring to legal counsel who can evaluate your specific corporate structure.

**If packaging is sold through a distributor that doesn't register or pay fees, does responsibility get pushed one layer up?**
This gets at what's called the producer hierarchy, and it's genuinely complicated. All covered packaging is supposed to be reported by someone, but exactly who requires a case-by-case analysis, the more complex your supply chain, the more complicated the answer. Nothing should be double-reported, and everything covered should be reported by at least one entity. As a rough rule of thumb, the brand whose logo is on the packaging is typically the obligated party, though that's an oversimplification, not a fully reliable test on its own. You may need to look further up the chain depending on importer status and how the packaging actually moves through the supply chain. CAA is actively interested in identifying non-compliant producers, so if you're seeing a case where the hierarchy isn't being respected, you can report that to CAA. If you're in a dispute with a supply-chain partner over who's responsible, that's a good situation to get legal support for.

**If a brand's logo isn't on the product itself but is on the outer case, does that make the case owner responsible?**
Generally speaking, and this can vary enough that complex supply chains should get specific legal guidance, each packaging layer is attributable to its own producer rather than the outermost logo being solely responsible. If chocolate bars carry the chocolate brand's logo, that brand carries EPR responsibility for that primary packaging. If those bars ship in a case bearing different branding, say, a box manufacturer's, that outer case very likely carries a separate obligation belonging to whoever commissioned that layer. It's not just whichever logo is on the outermost layer: each layer of packaging is generally attributable to whoever commissioned it.

**How should white label, private label, or private brand products be handled?**
In the vast majority of cases, the owner of the brand is ultimately responsible, including for white label, private label, and private brand products. As a general rule of thumb, the logo listed on the packaging is a good starting point for identifying that owner. Confirming the specifics of your own arrangement is still worth doing, since setups can vary.

## Looking Ahead

**Is there any hope for consistent EPR rules across states?**
A single federal policy would be simpler, but full uniformity would strip states of the ability to address their own needs: geography, infrastructure, and economics all differ, and California's challenges look very different from a smaller or more rural state's. That said, harmonization efforts are underway, including the federal PACK Act, which seeks a national standard for what counts as recyclable. States and organizations like CAA are also learning from each other as programs roll out, and newer states are drawing on lessons from Oregon, Colorado, and California. There won't likely be one uniform rulebook, but expect gradual convergence and easier implementation as more states stand up programs.
