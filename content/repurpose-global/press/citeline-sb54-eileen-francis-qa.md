With the August 1 Individual Source Reduction Plan deadline now imminent, what are the most common compliance mistakes you are seeing consumer brands make, and what should companies be doing right now if they have not yet started?

The most common mistake is treating this as a data project you can rush into in the final weeks. A credible ISR Plan needs a clean 2023 baseline, and for beauty and personal care brands specifically, that baseline has to track two things equally: plastic weight and component count. Brands that have only been watching weight are finding out late that component count carries the same exposure.

If you haven't started, the honest answer is to start with what you have, instead of waiting for what you wish you had. Pull your current packaging bill of materials, flag the SKUs where data is incomplete, and file a plan that's accurate about where those gaps are rather than one that guesses to look complete. An honest, partial baseline holds up better under review than a polished one built on assumptions.

Walk me through how a company should approach estimating and building out its Source Reduction Plan — what data do they need, how do they set realistic reduction targets, and what does a credible plan actually look like on paper?

Start with the data. The target follows from it, not the other way around. You need a complete 2023 baseline: every SKU, every component, material type, and weight. Without that, any reduction target you set is a guess dressed up as a plan.

From there, realistic targets come from mapping your packaging portfolio against the five approved pathways: material switching, lightweighting, PCR content, elimination, and reuse or refill. Not every SKU fits every pathway, and a credible plan says so explicitly rather than forcing a fit. If you've already made reductions before 2023, document that history in the plan's qualitative sections. It won't retroactively count toward the baseline, but it shows regulators you're not starting from zero on intent, even where the numbers still have to.

On paper, a credible plan reads less like a pledge and more like an audit: specific SKUs, specific pathways, specific numbers, and an honest account of where you're not yet compliant and why.

SB 54 requires producers to reduce single-use plastic by 25% by 2032 — how are brands balancing that sustainability mandate against the real cost and operational pressures of reformulating or redesigning packaging?

There's pressure on both sides, and pretending otherwise doesn't help anyone. Reformulating a package can mean new tooling, new supplier relationships, and up-front cost, on a timeline the brand didn't choose.

What we're seeing work is treating the reduction target as a portfolio decision instead of a per-SKU scramble. Brands that can model the fee and compliance impact of a packaging change before committing tooling budget to it are the ones finding pathways that hit the target without gutting margin, because they're comparing options instead of guessing at one. That's the exact gap our Packaging Simulator was built to close: turning a packaging decision from a spreadsheet guess into a side-by-side simulation to see EPR fees, compliance, and recyclability trade-offs before anything ships. The brands still treating this purely as a compliance cost, without modeling the packaging side of the equation, are the ones most likely to end up reformulating twice.

The law covers 95 material categories with no de minimis exclusions and requires detailed component-level data — how are personal care companies managing that reporting complexity, and where do you typically see data gaps?

There's no de minimis exclusion, so every component gets weighed and categorized, down to a cap, a liner, a pump mechanism. For personal care brands specifically, that's where the complexity compounds: a single SKU can carry five or six separate reportable components, each one a separate line in your data.

The gap we see most is component-level data simply not existing in one place. Plastic weight by material type is often tracked somewhere, usually finance or procurement, but component counts, especially for smaller parts like closures and applicators, are frequently undocumented anywhere formal. Brands end up reconstructing that data from supplier spec sheets months after the fact — the exact scramble a single, authoritative packaging data record is meant to prevent.

For beauty and personal care brands specifically, what packaging formats or materials are proving most difficult to bring into compliance, and what alternatives are brands turning to?

The hardest formats are the small, multi-material ones: ampoules, single-use applicator trays, sample sachets. Recyclability determinations in California weren't built with those shapes and material mixes in mind, and current sorting equipment often can't separate or process them even when a material is technically recyclable on its own.

Delivery systems are the harder redesign, rather than the easier one. Pump mechanisms, airless dispensers, precision applicators define premium positioning in this category, and they're difficult to reformulate quickly; a format change can take a year and a half to three years. What we're seeing instead is brands moving on secondary and transport packaging first, since eliminating or right-sizing an outer carton or a gift-with-purchase insert is a faster, cleaner win, while the harder primary-packaging and delivery-system redesigns get scoped on a longer timeline.

CalRecycle has already gone through multiple rounds of regulatory revision under SB 54 — how should brands think about building compliance programs that can absorb future regulatory changes without having to start over?

The programs that survive a regulation shift are the ones built around the data, not the current version of the rule. If your packaging data lives in one authoritative place, by SKU, by component, with material and weight tracked consistently, a regulatory revision just changes how you interpret that data. It doesn't send you back to collect it from scratch.

A program built only to answer this year's specific requirement has to start over with each revision. CalRecycle has already revised guidance multiple times, and it will again. Build for the data you'll always need regardless of what the current rule says, and the next revision is a re-read, not a re-do.

Similar EPR packaging laws are gaining traction in other states — to what extent is SB 54 effectively setting a national compliance standard, and should brands be building their packaging strategies with that broader regulatory trajectory in mind?

Functionally, yes, and it's less about SB 54 itself than about how packaging gets made. Very few brands manufacture state-specific packaging, so the practical floor becomes whichever state asks for the most, and today that's California.

What's different now is that California is no longer the only state asking. Oregon, Colorado, Maryland, Washington, Maine, and Minnesota all have their own EPR frameworks in some stage of rollout, each with its own categories, deadlines, and reporting mechanics that don't map onto each other cleanly. The brands handling this well didn't optimize for the best California plan. They built their packaging data once, at the component level, in a way that answers whatever a state asks next. That's the strategic bet: build for the data discipline instead of one law, because the next state won't wait for you to catch up the way this one did.
