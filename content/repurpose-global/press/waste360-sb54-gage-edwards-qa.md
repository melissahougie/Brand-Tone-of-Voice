From your work with consumer brands, what are the biggest compliance challenges companies are running into as they implement SB 54's packaging requirements?

The most common compliance challenge is data readiness. A credible ISR Plan needs a clean 2023 baseline and cross-functional analysis of packaging change opportunities for your packaging portfolio.

You can't build a credible plan without a clean 2023 baseline and data on potential packaging changes, but packaging data is usually scattered across supplier portals, spreadsheets, and co-manufacturer records with no single source of truth. We saw this firsthand processing hundreds of EPR reports this season.

The second gap is pathway analysis: brands have ideas but calculating the impact of packaging changes and mapping them to California's five approved pathways takes real upfront work.

The third gap, and the most consequential one long-term, is modeling the true financial impact. The CAA has published illustrative EPR fees and bonus-malus schedules. A plan built without that financial lens will surprise you later, and surprises in a program with $50,000-per-day penalties get expensive fast.

Most brands are somewhere in the middle of all three right now. Partial progress on the baseline, paired with an honest financial model, puts you in a far stronger position than waiting until everything's perfect to file. That's the work we do with brands directly: baseline reconciliation, pathway mapping, building the financial model with our Packaging Simulator. Anyone can reach out at repurpose.global.

What are some of the most common misconceptions or mistakes you've seen companies make while preparing their source reduction plans and long-term compliance strategies?

The biggest misconception is that the 25% target applies to individual producers. In reality, the 25% reduction target is CAA's obligation. For your individual source reduction plan, producers should ground in a realistic plan that's specific to their business – put a plan together that you believe is feasible, instead of trying to hit an arbitrary target.

A realistic timeline treats 2032 as a staged target, not a single swap-out event. Your ISR Plan is a projection for the future based on the current data available, not an exact roadmap that you're locked into. As such, you should incorporate realistic targets based on today's available packaging, but in your plan you can describe enablement factors (e.g. specific component / material formats and feasibility requirements) that would enable you to reduce further. These inputs will help CAA and Calrecycle make system investments that enable all producers to reach more aggressive reduction targets.

Another common mistake is treating component count as a secondary concern behind plastic weight. There's no de minimis exclusion under SB 54, so every component, down to a cap, a liner, a pump mechanism, gets weighed and categorized, and those two metrics carry equal exposure and both have to trend down simultaneously against your 2023 baseline. Plastic weight is usually tracked somewhere, often in finance or procurement, but component counts, especially for smaller parts like closures and applicators, are frequently undocumented anywhere formal, and brands end up reconstructing that data from supplier spec sheets months after the fact. Brands that optimize for weight alone are building a plan with a blind spot.

A third mistake is assuming instinctive plastic swaps automatically generate pathway credit. Switching from rigid bottles to flexible pouches is a move many consumer brands have already made, but flexible plastics currently aren't recyclable in California, so that switch doesn't count. Lightweighting earns credit only when the resulting material stays recyclable.

On PCR: the credit is incremental only. If your brand was already using recycled content before 2023, you don't get credit for it, only the increase above your baseline year counts, and it has to be APR-certified.

SB 54 requires companies to rethink packaging from both an environmental and business perspective. How are brands balancing sustainability goals with cost, supply chain realities and operational feasibility?

California's fee structure is more complex than most brands realize. Beyond the base EPR fee, there's a reuse investment fee, a plastic pollution mitigation fund fee tied to plastic weight and component count, and a bonus-malus system that rewards progress and penalizes laggards with escalating fees through 2032. Most brands walking in only see the base fee.

The CAA has published an illustrative fee and bonus-malus schedule. Brands that can model the fee and compliance impact of a packaging change before committing tooling budget to it are the ones finding pathways that hit the target without gutting margin, because they're comparing options instead of guessing at one. That's the exact gap our Packaging Simulator is built to close: turning a packaging decision from a spreadsheet guess into a side-by-side simulation of EPR fees, compliance, and recyclability trade-offs before anything ships. With $50,000-per-day penalties, delay is its own cost driver.

Beyond the August 1 deadline, companies will face annual reporting and ongoing compliance requirements through 2032. What should organizations be doing now to build a long-term compliance strategy rather than treating this as a one-time exercise?

Compliance is a financial strategy, not just a filing: model the fees, bonuses, and maluses before you commit to a packaging change, not after.

The compliance strategies that survive a regulation shift are the ones built around the data, not the current version of the rule. If your packaging data lives in one authoritative place, by SKU, by component, with material and weight tracked consistently, a regulatory revision just changes how you interpret that data. It doesn't send you back to collect it from scratch.

CalRecycle has already revised guidance multiple times, and it will again. Build for the data you'll always need regardless of what the current rule says, and the next revision is a re-read, not a re-do.

Many companies sell products nationwide rather than exclusively in California. How is SB 54 influencing packaging decisions outside the state, and are you seeing brands standardize packaging across multiple markets?

Packaging is produced as a standard across states; manufacturing state-specific packaging is very difficult to do. That makes California the floor for the whole portfolio, regardless of company size.

Most brands have already accepted this and are designing to California as the de facto national standard. SB 343's truth-in-labeling requirements make that harder to sidestep too: a recyclability claim change on pack flows through the entire supply chain, not just the California-bound units.

Several states have adopted or are considering extended producer responsibility (EPR) laws for packaging.

What lessons from California do you think will shape future packaging regulations across the U.S., and what should companies be watching next?

Oregon, Colorado, Maryland, Washington, Maine, Minnesota: state-level EPR isn't a California story anymore, and brands treating it as one are accumulating risk.

The lesson that applies everywhere is data first. If your packaging data lives in one authoritative place, by SKU, by component, with material and weight tracked consistently, a regulatory revision just changes how you interpret that data. It doesn't send you back to collect it from scratch. Brands with clean, audit-ready data respond to new frameworks in weeks. Brands that haven't will repeat the scramble we're seeing in California right now.

At the federal level, SB 54's core idea, that producers have to actually reduce plastic at the source rather than just fund recycling, is already shaping how policymakers think about producer responsibility. Brands that treat regulation as a forcing function for better packaging decisions are the ones building a real competitive advantage: they've stopped modeling packaging decisions in spreadsheets and started simulating the full compliance and fee impact before anything ships.

Looking ahead, what advice would you give to brands that want to move beyond simply meeting regulatory requirements and instead use packaging compliance as an opportunity to drive innovation and strengthen their sustainability strategy?

The brands getting real value out of this aren't treating the ISR Plan as a one-time filing. They're treating the underlying data, what's in each package, what it weighs, how it's classified, as something built once and used everywhere: the same baseline that satisfies California also feeds Scope 3 reporting, answers the product-level questions retailers are increasingly asking, and gives R&D a real basis for comparing packaging formats before committing to one.

That data discipline pays off in marketing too: a sustainability claim only holds up if there's real documentation behind it, and a compliance-grade baseline already is that documentation.

California's own bonus-malus system rewards measurable improvement financially, not just penalizes the lack of it, so the same investment that keeps you compliant can also make a business case internally.

Not to mention, California will require annual source reduction progress reporting to financially incentivize real source reduction results. That means this ISR Plan is just the tip of the spear. There will be ongoing measurement and evaluation, and financial ROI over the coming years that will give compliance and sustainability teams new ammunition to make sustainable packaging changes.

The advice is simple: build the data infrastructure once, then use it for every question it can answer, compliance, claims, retailer requests, product decisions, instead of rebuilding it from scratch every time a new one comes up. Build for the data you'll always need regardless of what the current rule says, and the next revision becomes a re-read, not a re-do.
