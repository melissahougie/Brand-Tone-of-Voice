Q: With California's August 1 source reduction plan deadline just weeks away, what are the most common compliance gaps you're seeing among brands, and what last-minute steps can companies still take to meet the requirements?

The most common gap is data readiness. The ISR Plan is a binding legal commitment, and you can't build a credible plan without a clean 2023 baseline — but packaging data is scattered across supplier portals, spreadsheets, and co-manufacturer records with no single source of truth. We saw this firsthand processing hundreds of EPR reports this season.

The second gap is pathway attribution: brands have made packaging changes but haven't mapped them to California's five approved pathways yet — and that mapping requires real upfront work to get credit.

The third gap, and most consequential long-term: modeling the true financial impact. The CAA has published illustrative EPR fees and bonus-malus schedules. A plan built without that financial lens will surprise you later — and surprises in a program with $50,000-per-day penalties tend to be expensive.

If brands are feeling behind, they're not alone — this is exactly where most brands are right now. The most important thing is to start moving. Even partial progress on the baseline, combined with an honest financial model puts you in a significantly stronger position than waiting for everything to be perfect before filing.

Q: SB 54 is considered one of the most comprehensive packaging regulations in U.S. history – can you break down the specific source reduction targets and reporting requirements that are catching brands off guard?

A few things consistently catch brands off guard. The baseline is fixed — 2023 plastic weight and component count are permanently locked in, with no adjustment for business growth. Both metrics must trend down simultaneously; you can't trade one against the other.

The pathway mix matters more than most expect. At least net 10% out of the 25% total reduction target by 2032 must come from reuse, refill, or elimination. PCR alone won't get you there — it's capped at 8% and must be APR-certified.

The curveball most miss: California won't recognize source reduction that results in less recyclable packaging. Lightweighting through flexible formats, an instinctive move, doesn't count as long as flexible plastics aren't recyclable in California.

Finally, the sequencing surprised many: the annual source reduction report on 2025 activity was due May 31, before the forward-looking ISR Plan due August 1. The significance of that May deadline caught a lot of brands off guard.

Q: How are brands currently modeling the financial impact of SB 54 compliance, and what are the biggest cost drivers – material substitution, redesign, reporting infrastructure, or something else entirely?

California's fee structure is more complex than most brands realize. Beyond the base EPR fee, there's a reuse investment fee, a plastic pollution mitigation fund fee tied to plastic weight and component count, and a bonus-malus system that rewards progress and penalizes laggards with escalating fees through 2032. Most brands walking in only see the base fee.

The CAA has published an illustrative fee and bonus-malus schedule. Baking those figures into your ISR Plan — and modeling the impact of different packaging scenarios before any change ships — is what turns compliance from a cost center into a packaging optimization opportunity. With $50,000-per-day penalties and a 10% reduction milestone in January 2027, delay is its own cost driver.

Q: For plastics manufacturers and packaging converters, how is SB 54 changing the conversations they're having with brand customers, and what capabilities are brands now demanding from their supply chain partners?

The conversation has completely changed. Brands used to come to suppliers with a spec. Now they're coming with a question: what does this packaging cost me in California terms? What's the plastic weight per unit? What PCR is available for this resin, and is it APR-certifiable?

Suppliers who can answer those questions are pulling ahead. Accurate material weight by component, PCR sourcing with chain-of-custody documentation, and the ability to model lightweighting options without a 12-month tooling cycle are becoming table stakes for retaining brand customers in California. Suppliers who understand California's pathway framework are becoming strategic advisors.

Q: Beyond California, how is SB 54 influencing packaging strategies nationally – are brands designing for California compliance and applying it everywhere, or taking a state-by-state approach?

Packaging is produced as a standard across states — it's very challenging to manufacture state-specific packaging. That makes California the floor for the whole portfolio, regardless of company size.

Most brands have accepted this and are already designing to California as a de facto national standard. In addition, SB 343's truth-in-labeling requirements make California hard to sidestep — recyclability claim changes on pack flow through the entire supply chain.

Q: What role is material innovation playing in compliance strategies – are brands gravitating toward specific plastic alternatives, recycled content solutions, or lightweighting approaches to meet source reduction goals?

PCR, elimination of excess plastic components and lightweighting are the most immediate moves, because they fit within existing infrastructure, don't require packaging redesign from scratch, and can generate compliance credit relatively quickly. PCR in particular is being treated as near-term low-hanging fruit, especially for rigid formats like bottles, trays, and closures in PET, HDPE, and PP as many other packaging laws also put PCR content mandates e.g. California, Washington, Maine etc.. The ceiling matters, though — California caps PCR collectively at 8% credit, and only incremental PCR above your 2023 baseline counts. Brands that already incorporated PCR before the baseline year don't get credit for it.

Lightweighting is also where I see the most active work right now. Down-gauging films, thinner walls and closures, simplified laminate structures; these are established approaches that procurement and packaging teams know how to execute, provided it doesn't move a material from recyclable to non-recyclable. The challenge is in the documentation: every gram of reduction has to be attributed to an approved pathway with verifiable data.

Material switching, like moving to paper, glass, or metal, is also happening, but the timelines are long. A complex format change can take 18 to 36 months.

Q: You mentioned that source reduction reporting will become an increasingly important business function — what infrastructure and expertise do companies need to build now to stay ahead of evolving regulations beyond California?

The companies that will navigate this landscape well are building three things. First, a single authoritative source for packaging data versus spreadsheets scattered across teams. A centralized system that captures plastic weight, component count, material type, and PCR content by SKU, and keeps it current as packaging changes. That data is the foundation of every report, every plan, every compliance decision.

Second, cross-functional ownership. Source reduction is a team sport. It touches packaging design, procurement, R&D, legal, finance, and supply chain. The brands I see handling this best have a steering group with actual decision-making authority across those functions. The ISR Plan is a binding legal agreement; it can't be assembled by one person in a silo.

Third, the ability to model before you commit. Regulations will continue to evolve. Brands that can rapidly simulate the compliance and cost impact of packaging changes before those changes go to tooling will make faster, smarter decisions. That's not just a California capability. That's the infrastructure for navigating whatever comes next.

Q: Looking at the broader regulatory landscape, what other state or federal packaging regulations should the plastics industry be preparing for, and how can lessons from SB 54 compliance inform those strategies?

Oregon, Colorado, Maryland, Washington, Maine, Minnesota — state-level EPR is no longer a California story, and the brands treating it as one are accumulating risk.

The lesson that applies everywhere: data first. Every EPR framework is built on your packaging baseline — what you put into market, what it weighs, what it's made of. Brands with clean, audit-ready data respond to new frameworks in weeks. Brands that haven't will repeat the scramble we're seeing in California right now.

At the federal level, SB 54's core idea — that producers must actually reduce plastic at the source, not just fund recycling — is already shaping how policymakers think about producer responsibility. The brands that treat regulation as a forcing function for better packaging decisions will build a genuine competitive advantage.
