unPacked: Filed Isn't Finished — What's Next for EPR in Q3

1. Cheryl Baldwin (1:17 PM)
Q: Can you provide a description and examples of non-compliant packaging for Maine that you referenced in the timeline?
A: Lowell clarified this wasn't about specific packaging types being outlawed — it's about non-compliant producers. Maine's law allows the state to bar a liable producer (one selling covered packaging above the revenue and packaging thresholds) from selling into the state if that producer hasn't registered, reported, or paid the required fees, as most EPR programs reserve this kind of enforcement mechanism. There's enough clarity in Maine's rules today to determine whether you're a liable producer and get your data and internal processes in order so you don't become non-compliant.

For comparison, revisions to previous reports are allowed in Colorado and Oregon, so there's a reasonable chance CA follows suit eventually — but there's no clarity yet on timing or process.

11. Brad Bowers (2:01 PM)
Q: For small business exemptions, irrespective of how you registered with the CAA, is that evaluated at the parent level or each subsidiary level? Oregon defines it at the parent level but other states it is not clear.
A: This is a legal question we can't advise on directly, and it does vary significantly by state. California, for example, offers different options for claiming a small business exemption at either the subsidiary or the group/parent level, but the right approach depends on how your company is incorporated (tax ID/CIN structure) and how your packaging supply chain and sales actually work. Because both the rules and the underlying business facts can be unclear, this is a good one to bring to legal counsel who can evaluate your specific corporate structure.

12. Enzo M (2:02 PM)
Q: Is there any hope for consistent rules among states?
A: While a single federal policy would be simpler, full uniformity would strip states of the ability to address their own unique needs — geography, infrastructure, economics, etc. (California's challenges are very different from a smaller or more rural state's). That said, harmonization efforts are underway, including things like the federal PACK Act, which seeks a national standard for what's considered recyclable. States and organizations like CAA are also learning from each other as programs roll out — the goal isn't to make things maximally difficult for producers, but to address each state's specific needs, and newer states are drawing on lessons learned from Oregon, Colorado, and California's rollouts. So while there won't likely be one uniform rulebook, expect gradual convergence and easier implementation as more states stand up programs.

15. Charlie Satossky (2:16 PM)
Q: How do you foresee the states enforcing packaging sold through a distributor that does not register or pay fees? Would the producer in these cases ever be pushed one layer up for accountability?
A: This gets at what's called the producer hierarchy, and it's genuinely complicated. All covered packaging is supposed to be reported by someone, but exactly who that is requires a case-by-case analysis — the more complex your supply chain, the more complicated the answer. Nothing should be double-reported, and everything covered should be reported by at least one entity. As a rough rule of thumb, the brand whose logo is on the packaging is typically the obligated party, though that's an oversimplification and not a fully reliable way to assess obligation on its own — you may need to look further up the chain depending on importer status and how the packaging actually moves through the supply chain; obligation can sometimes shift upstream depending on how the business is set up. CAA is actively interested in identifying non-compliant producers and bringing them into compliance so everyone pays their fair share, so if you're seeing a case where the obligation hierarchy isn't being respected, you can report that to CAA. If you're in a dispute with a supply-chain partner over who's responsible, that's a good situation to get legal support for.

16. Sarah Sneed (2:22 PM)
Q: What if brand logos are not on the plastic product, but on the corrugated case the product is packaged in? Would any branding deem that party responsible for EPR fees?
A: Generally speaking (this can vary, so complex supply chains should get specific legal guidance), each packaging layer is attributable to its own producer rather than the outermost logo being solely responsible. For example, if chocolate bars carry the chocolate brand's logo, that brand carries EPR responsibility for that primary packaging; if those bars ship in a case bearing different branding (say, a box manufacturer's), that outer case very likely carries a separate obligation belonging to whoever commissioned that layer. So it's not just whichever logo is on the outermost layer — each layer of packaging is generally attributable to whoever commissioned it, based on the branding that appears on that specific layer.

17. Enzo M (2:22 PM)
Q: Can you quickly cover how white label/private label/private brand should be handled? It is my understanding that the owner of the brand is ultimately responsible. Can you dive into that a little?
A: In the vast majority of cases, it's the owner of the brand that is ultimately responsible, including for white label, private label, and private brand products. As a general (oversimplified) rule of thumb, the logo listed on the packaging is a good starting point — that's typically the brand owner whose responsibility it is. For your specific situation, it's worth confirming the details since arrangements can vary.
